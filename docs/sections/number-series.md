# Number and letter series

**In one line:** you see a list of numbers or letters that follows a rule, and you pick what comes next.

Series questions sit inside the math and logic type that Criteria lists for the CCAT. They need very little arithmetic. What they need is a fast, ordered way of testing rules, so that you find the pattern in seconds instead of staring at it.

## What it measures

A series item checks whether you can spot a rule from a few examples and apply it once more. That is a small version of what Criteria says the CCAT is for: learning something new from limited information and using it straight away.

## How the questions look

A row of four to seven terms, a blank at the end, and five options. Most rules fall into a short list:

| Rule | Example | Next |
|---|---|---|
| Add or subtract the same number | 3, 7, 11, 15 | 19 |
| Multiply or divide by the same number | 2, 6, 18, 54 | 162 |
| Differences that grow steadily | 5, 8, 14, 23, 35 (gaps 3, 6, 9, 12) | 50 |
| Squares or cubes | 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 | 36 |
| Each term is the sum of the two before | 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 | 21 |
| Two steps that alternate | 4, 10, 7, 13, 10 (+6, −3) | 16 |
| Two series woven together | 1, 20, 3, 18, 5, 16 | 7 |
| Multiply then add | 2, 5, 11, 23 (× 2 + 1) | 47 |
| Letters by alphabet position | A, C, F, J (gaps 2, 3, 4) | O |

The last row is why the alphabet numbers are worth knowing by heart: A = 1, E = 5, J = 10, O = 15, T = 20, Z = 26. With those anchors you can find any letter in a second or two.

## A method that works

Test rules in this order. Most items fall at step 1 or 2.

1. **Write the gaps.** Subtract each term from the next. If the gaps are equal, add the gap once more.
2. **If the gaps are not equal, look at the gaps themselves.** Do they grow by a fixed amount (3, 6, 9, 12)? Do they double? Then the gaps have their own series.
3. **If the numbers grow fast, try multiplying.** Divide each term by the one before. A constant ratio (× 3) settles it.
4. **If the gaps go up and down, split the series.** Read terms 1, 3, 5 as one list and terms 2, 4, 6 as another. Or look for two steps that alternate.
5. **Check for known sequences:** squares (1, 4, 9, 16), cubes (1, 8, 27, 64), primes (2, 3, 5, 7, 11) and the add-the-last-two pattern.
6. **Try "multiply then add".** Each term is twice the one before plus one, or three times minus two, and so on.
7. **Check your rule on every term,** not just the last two. A rule that fits three terms but breaks on the fourth is the wrong rule.

## Traps that cost points

- **Fitting the rule to the last two terms only.** Many options are built so that a rule which fits only the end of the series gives a wrong answer that looks right.
- **Missing the split series.** If a series zigzags (20, 3, 18, 5, 16), it is almost always two series woven together.
- **Miscounting letters.** Count on your fingers or use the anchors (E = 5, J = 10, O = 15, T = 20). Skipping a letter is the most common letter-series mistake.
- **Stopping at the first pattern you see.** 2, 4, 8 could be "double" or "add 2, then 4". Check the next term to decide.
- **Spending too long.** If the first five steps of the method find nothing, guess. An unusual rule is rarely worth 40 seconds.

## Timing

As guidance: aim for 15 to 25 seconds per series item. Steps 1 and 2 take five seconds and solve most items. If you reach step 6 with no rule, choose the option that best fits the overall size and direction of the series, and move on.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the CCAT or from Criteria's materials. Find the next term.

**Item 1.** 5, 8, 14, 23, 35, ?

A. 44 · B. 47 · C. 48 · D. 50 · E. 52

**Item 2.** 4, 10, 7, 13, 10, ?

A. 7 · B. 13 · C. 16 · D. 17 · E. 19

**Item 3.** 1, 20, 3, 18, 5, 16, ?

A. 6 · B. 7 · C. 9 · D. 14 · E. 15

**Item 4.** A, C, F, J, ?

A. M · B. N · C. O · D. P · E. Q

**Item 5.** 2, 5, 11, 23, 47, ?

A. 71 · B. 89 · C. 94 · D. 95 · E. 96

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 5</summary>

**Item 1: D, 50.** The gaps are 3, 6, 9, 12, growing by 3 each time. The next gap is 15, and 35 + 15 = 50. *Why not B:* 47 adds the last gap (12) again.

**Item 2: C, 16.** The steps alternate: +6, −3, +6, −3. The next step is +6, and 10 + 6 = 16. *Why not A:* 7 applies −3 again, but −3 was the last step, so it is +6's turn.

**Item 3: B, 7.** Two series are woven together. Terms 1, 3 and 5 are 1, 3, 5, going up by 2. Terms 2, 4 and 6 are 20, 18, 16, going down by 2. The seventh term belongs to the first series: 5 + 2 = 7. *Why not D:* 14 is the next term of the second series, which is one place too far.

**Item 4: C, O.** In alphabet positions the series is 1, 3, 6, 10. The gaps are 2, 3, 4, so the next gap is 5: 10 + 5 = 15, which is O. *Why not B:* N is 14, one letter short, which is the usual counting slip.

**Item 5: D, 95.** Each term is double the one before plus one: 2 × 2 + 1 = 5, 5 × 2 + 1 = 11, and 47 × 2 + 1 = 95. *Why not C:* 94 doubles without adding one. *Why not A:* 71 adds the last gap (24) again.

- **5:** series are a strength. Keep them fast; you should be under 20 seconds each.
- **4:** note which rule type you missed. Most people miss the split series (item 3) or the multiply-then-add type (item 5). Add them to your checklist.
- **3 or fewer:** learn the method order by heart and use it on every item until it is automatic. Practise writing the gaps first, every time.
